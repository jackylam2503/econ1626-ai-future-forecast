The Algorithmic Supermarket

The supermarket industry is on the verge of its most significant transformation since the introduction to barcode scanning. By 2027-2030, AI will completely reshape how Australians will shop, how they operate, as well as, how new value will be created and distributed across the entire grocery supply chain. Technology like AI powered demand forecasting, to agentic commerce that will anticipate what consumers need before they even know it themselves. That type of technology shouldn't be a future consideration anymore, it is already being deployed and the gap between those who accept and adapt and those who do not is widening exponentially.

This thesis will argue that AI will force a productivity revolution within the Australian supermarket industry. It will explore how AI can deliver measurable gains in efficiency, reduce waste, and enhance consumer experience. However, without deliberate policy intervention, the competitive advantages of AI will dwindle in comparison to the risks it can impose. The question is not whether AI will reshape this industry, as it definitely will, rather, it is whether Australia's policy and governance frameworks can ensure that the gains be shared broadly.

Section 01

Capabilities → Economic Channels

1.1 The Customer Discovery Shift

Customers are no longer browsing shelves, instead, they are consulting AI on what to purchase before even setting foot in a store. According to the Klaviyo 2025 AI shopping index, 86% of Australians have used AI tools for online shopping in the past 3 months (Ignacio 2025), surpassing the global average by 10 percentage points. Furthermore, 43% of Australians use AI regularly for shopping decisions, and 39% use it to guide what they buy (Duong 2026). This represents a shift in the economic mechanism of consumer demand, the purchase decision is no longer happening in the aisles, they're happening at home, on a screen, before the trip is even planned.

The implications for supermarkets are significant. Whenever AI recommends a product, what happens is that it completely bypasses all the traditional marketing channels, shelf placement, in store promotions, etc, are essentially made redundant. Therefore, supermarkets are no longer just competing within traditional channels, instead, they need to consider their ability to appear favourably within AI recommendation systems. A Cognizant and Oxford economics study projects that 55% of Australian consumer transactions will be influenced by AI by 2030, representing an estimated $669 billion in AI influenced spending (Writer 2025). A retailer failing to optimise for this new layer risks losing relevance before their customers even enter their store.

1.2 Predictive Shopping & Agentic Commerce

The next layer of AI in supermarkets explores an idea that goes beyond influencing what customers buy, it is beginning to execute purchases on their behalf. Looking at the overseas market, Walmart partnered with OpenAI to allow customers to complete purchases entirely within ChatGPT. This initiative, agentic commerce, highlights the capabilities of AI and explores how it can move from reactive to proactive. Picture AI knowing pasta night is on every Friday and ordering the ingredients before you have even thought about it. This is no longer a far fetched theory, it is already being tested and deployed.

When consumer demands are anticipated and purchases are made autonomous, it restructures demand. Impulsive chocolate bar purchases at the counter, eye-catching promotional items, all these may be at risk as the entire shopping journey of a consumer is compressed into automation. Whether this may be a good or bad thing is a whole other argument, but the fact that it will reshape what traditional demand means cannot be ignored. Analysts suggest that agentic commerce has the capacity to transform not just consumer retail but B2B supply chains, demand planning, and customer service across the entire industry (Wang & Tan 2025). This isn't about engaging with agentic commerce, it is about building the data infrastructure to compete in it.

1.3 Operations & Supply Chain Efficiency

AI can now optimise everything from logistics to the supply chain. It can improve efficiency within operations in ways such as; improving forecasting accuracy, inventory management and eliminating unnecessary labour. The Financial impact that AI has on global retail is on a steep incline. Global retail AI is growing from $11 billion in 2024 to a predicted $40 billion by 2030. That is a 23% annual growth which is a growth faster than any other business category. This shows that the sentiment of AI within the markets and investors is strong and AI development is being rewarded.

Moreover, demand forecasting plays a big role in business operations and AI can work to improve this system. According to a study AI-driven forecasting reduces prediction errors by 20-50% which can translate to 5-10% lower warehousing costs, up to 65% reduction in lost sales from stockouts, and 25-40% improvement in admin costs. All these reductions may look marginal, but at a scale, cost savings can be accumulated, and reinvested into further areas.

Walmart is a good example of how AI deployed within the supply chain can improve optimisation and reduce costs. When they rolled out their AI, they documented a 16% reduction in stockouts and improved shelf fill rates. Additionally they also eliminated 30 million unnecessary trucking miles annually which shows a significant margin improvement and sustainability. These efficiency gains compound over time, larger retailers like Coles and Woolworths who have the capacity to invest in AI infrastructure early, will accumulate cost advantages that smaller supermarkets cannot match.

1.4 Labour & Employment Effects

AI's efficiency gains don't come without human cost. The biggest roles at risk are those built around routine and repetition; checkout employees, inventory clerks, warehousing staff. McKinsey estimates that by 2030, 1.3 million Australian workers may need to transition into entirely new roles due to automation and AI. This is further reinforced by Jobs and Skills Australia, finding that administrative and routine roles face the greatest exposure to augmented work. For supermarkets, by 2030 the workforce will look different. Their roles will be displaced and the adequacy of reskilling investment will decide whether those workers are transitioned smoothly or left behind.

1.5 Market Structure & Concentration

AI does not just change how supermarkets operate, they also can change who can compete. The efficiency gains, data advantages, and infrastructure required to deploy AI at a scale inherently favour larger businesses. In Australia, this raises a slight concern. Coles and Woolworths already control roughly 65% of the grocery market. AI can risk compounding the duopoly dominance further, as smaller retailers will lack the capital to match that duopoly. What is initially an operational advantage becomes a structural barrier to entry, which can potentially launch Australia's supermarket landscape into an even more concentrated market by 2030.

Section 02

Scenarios: 2027–2030

The road for AI in Australia’s supermarket industry is not fixed. How it can unfold by 2030 will depend on three key variables; pace of AI adoption, strength of regulator response, and willingness of the industry to invest in workforce transitions.

↑ Upside

AI adoption is rapid and broadly accessible. Policy keeps pace, workers are supported, and productivity gains are shared across the industry.

→ Baseline

AI adoption is uneven. The duopoly pulls ahead, regulation lags, and benefits are concentrated rather than shared.

↓ Downside

Policy fails to respond. Independent retailers exit, job losses mount, and algorithmic pricing goes unchecked.

Within an upside scenario, AI adoption across Australia's supermarket industry is rapid, well managed, and broadly accessible. It will begin with Coles and Woolworths leading the charge, but it will also be widely affordable to smaller independent grocers to minimise the concentration of the market. Areas like productivity are improved, forecasting becomes more accurate with fewer errors, the supply chain tightens, and waste is significantly reduced. In light of these savings, it can reflect onto consumers through lower prices and improving their shopping experience especially at a time when the cost of living remains a key concern for many Australians.

Furthermore, the government and industry will move together. This will mean proactive investment into reskilling programs so that workers displaced by AI can transition into higher value positions. The ACCC will also be actively monitoring the algorithms, keeping the competition alive. By 2030, there will be a full transformation of the industry, depicting how the supermarket sector can deliver more efficiency and responsiveness when policy can keep pace with the growth of technology.

Within a baseline scenario, AI adoption will happen but will proceed unevenly. Coles and Woolworths will still be leading our industry, investing heavily, which will widen the gap between them and smaller independent grocers. Smaller grocers inevitably lack the capital needed to fund AI research and to properly roll out changes. The reality will be that productivity gains are indeed real, however, they will be only evident to the duopoly, rather than the broader industry.

Some labour displacements will occur, especially in routine roles, however, there will not be any major reskilling effort, ultimately, leaving those workers behind. The ACCC will monitor the market, but does not intervene proactively to minimise further concentration of the duopoly. Policy will catch up after the fact instead of ahead of it.

Consumers will also experience mixed results, shopping will become more convenient and personalised, however, the pricing power will lay firmly with Coles and Woolworths. By 2030, Australia's supermarket industry will look better in terms of efficiency and productivity, however, it will also be more concentrated, offering less alternatives for consumers and suppliers.

In the downside scenario, the risks that are outlined within this thesis will materialise without adequate policy response. To begin, AI will completely engulf the duopoly only. Coles and Woolworths use of AI will lock out the remaining competition, independent grocers will struggle and fizzle out of the market entirely. Similarly, there will be significant job losses, routine roles are automated faster than reskilling can keep up, leaving behind a trail of redundancies. The social cost of this displacement will affect disproportionately as lower class income workers are less equipped to adapt.

Moreover, algorithmic pricing can become exploitative. Consumers can be charged differently based on their personal data. This can also hurt smaller suppliers like farmers and producers, as there will be far less competition among retailers which in turn, leads to suppliers having less bargaining power. Implications of AI will be quick and evident in comparison to regulations. By the time regulations arrive, the market will be too concentrated to reverse. The promise of AI will cause a rift in the industry - a double edged sword, it has the capacity to reshape the industry but at what cost?

Section 03

Policy Playbook

3.1 Competition Policy

Australia's supermarket industry is already one of the most concentrated grocery markets in the world, and AI can run the risk of accelerating that concentration. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) is Australia's independent government authority that enforces competition and consumer protection laws. They must take a proactive role in how Coles and Woolworths deploy AI across their supply chain. The ACCC's 2024 supermarket inquiry already exposed pricing concerns and with the addition to AI, AI driven algorithmic pricing will add a new layer of complexity that current frameworks cannot handle.

The policy recommendation is mandatory algorithmic transparency. Large retailers must be required to disclose how AI influences their pricing decisions. This ensures that regulators are able to identify exploitative behaviours. Additionally, the government should also consider subsidising AI tool access so that independent grocers are able to enter easier to prevent further concentration.

3.2 Labour & Workforce Transition

The displacement of routine labour is a major concern regarding adoption of AI. AI will displace routine roles faster than the market can naturally absorb them. Without intervention the social cost falls entirely on individuals. Therefore, the recommendation is two approaches that tackle this issue. Firstly, the government should fund accessible reskilling programs specifically targeting potential displaced supermarket workers, allowing those individuals to equip themselves with the new skills to take on other roles. Secondly, there should be an industrial obligation for large retailers, Coles and Woolworths, to contribute to workforce transition funds. These recommendations come as a trade off, mandatory obligations will add cost to retailers and may slow down adoption. However, weighing out the alternative of mass redundancies among Australia's lowest income workers, the trade off carries far greater long term economic and social costs.

3.3 Algorithmic Pricing & Data Governance

Coles and Woolworths already collect vast amounts of consumer data through their programs like Flybuys and Everyday Rewards. As AI becomes more sophisticated, there is potential that this data can be used to implement personalised pricing. Assuming AI automates the shopping experience, this means two customers may be charged different prices based on their shopping history and data. Without proper regulations, this can become extremely exploitative.

The recommendation is two steps. First, retailers are mandated to disclose when their pricing is algorithmically determined. This will give consumers transparency. Secondly, there should be an independent body established to strictly monitor and review the AI pricing systems to identify and exploitative behaviour. The trade off is that having strict data regulations can add to compliance costs, however it is a necessity as without transparency, trust in a system can easily erode which carries its own long term economic costs.
